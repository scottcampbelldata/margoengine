# Particle liquid

`@margoengine/water/liquid` adds `ParticleLiquid`, an HDR `PostStack` stage that draws any set of particle centres as a liquid surface over the opaque scene. It renders only: positions come from the caller (an own SPH or FLIP solver, a recorded cache, a compute buffer). Use `ParticleLiquid.attach(engine, { particles: { positions }, radius })`, or `new ParticleLiquid(options)` inside a PostStack. A CPU `Float32Array` is uploaded at creation and on each `update()`; a `GPUBuffer` or `GpuArray` is read in place.

How it works. (1) Each particle is a quad through its centre facing the eye, sized to cover the sphere's silhouette cone; the fragment intersects the view ray with the sphere, tests the hit against the scene depth and writes the eye depth to an r32float target with depth testing (van der Laan, Green and Sainz 2009). (2) The narrow-range filter of Truong and Yuksel (2018) smooths that depth: neighbours behind by more than the dynamic low threshold are clamped to d + mu (eq. 2), a symmetric pair is dropped when either side is closer than the high threshold (bias correction, eq. 6), both thresholds widen along the surface (eqs. 7 to 9), and the kernel is 3 sigma_i pixels with sigma_i from a world-space sigma (eq. 5). It runs as a horizontal and a vertical pass plus the paper's 5 x 5 two-dimensional clean-up pass. Pixels outside the view contribute no pair, so the image border does not bend the surface. (3) Every sphere's chord through the view ray, cut at the scene depth, is added into an r16float thickness target. (4) The composite rebuilds view positions from the filtered depth, takes normals from the smaller one-sided difference (van der Laan et al.), refracts the view ray by Snell's law for the thickness and reads the scene where it leaves, absorbs it by exp(-absorption * thickness) per channel, and adds the exact unpolarised Fresnel reflection of the sky (Water's gradient or an equirectangular environment) and a normalised Blinn-Phong highlight of the engine light. All passes are render passes reading with textureLoad, without workgroup memory, barriers or 32-bit float blending, so the WebGL2 layer runs each as an ordinary draw.

Rival. Babylon.js FluidRenderer (Green's GDC 2010 bilateral pipeline) is the only built-in web rival; three.js and PlayCanvas have none. Margo's filter keeps silhouettes and does not bleed across depth gaps (a bilateral filter flattens drops and distorts the background next to them, Truong and Yuksel figure 3), refraction follows Snell's law through the thickness, absorption is per-channel Beer-Lambert with Water's coefficients, and both backends run it. Not built yet (the finish-later list): the narrow band of Oliveira and Paiva 2022 (boundary particles from a voxel grid, thickness from voxel centroids), a vfx particle-buffer adapter, and the diffuse and velocity outputs of the rival. Whitewater is built (below); the rival has none.

Proof (RTX A6000, native WebGPU and MARGO_COMPAT=1, both pass): packages/water/test/liquid.browser.mjs. The GPU filter matches the float64 port of the paper's equations (packages/water/test/liquid-reference.js) on the same raw depth at all 6,408 covered pixels within 1e-4 relative (worst 8.5e-7 native, 6.8e-7 compat). A 20,000-particle shell of radius 1 (particle radius 0.05) gives a mean filtered-depth error of 0.0040 against the analytic outer sphere (raw sprites 0.0014). The thickness through the centre of one sphere of radius 1 is 1.9990 against the exact chord 1.9997. Refraction lands on the analytically predicted scene texel within 1 texel at 196 sampled pixels, always toward the centre; Fresnel matches the exact formula within 0.0005. A two-radius gap between two spheres stays empty, and a wall behind a drop moves by at most 0.0073 (7% of the particle radius, from the dropped pairs) when the drop is added and is never pulled toward it. Steady frame: 6 render passes, 6 draws, 320 uniform bytes, no bind group, no texture or buffer created (also at 1,000,000 particles in 1920 x 1080); a CPU `update()` adds count * stride * 4 bytes once.

Cost (busy-machine diagnostics, A6000; packages/water/test/liquid-scale.browser.mjs): GPU time 1.05 ms for 32,768 particles at 1280 x 720, 1.70 ms for 262,144 at 1920 x 1080, 8.06 ms for 4,194,304 at 1920 x 1080. Targets and the quiet rerun: docs/work-lists/speed-phase.md.

## Whitewater

With `particles.velocities` and `whitewater` set, each `update()` runs one step of the diffuse-material model of Ihmsen, Akinci, Akinci and Teschner (2012) on the GPU, and every frame draws the pool.

How it works, per step, all render passes: (1) a neighbour grid of cells h / 2 wide that wraps every G cells (G a power of two, so it needs no domain bounds; z slices are tiled in 2D so the cells around a particle are neighbouring texels), filled as Harada, Koshizuka and Kawaguchi (2007) fill their bucket texture: one point draw per slot, eight slots per cell, the depth test keeping the smallest particle index above the previous slot. A neighbour found through a wrapped cell is skipped unless it lies in the cell being visited, so each counts once. (2) Surface normals. (3) Per particle the paper's potentials: trapped air sum_j |v_ij| (1 - v^_ij . x^_ij) W(x_ij, h) with W = 1 - |x| / h (eqs. 2 and 3), wave crest sum_j (1 - n^_i . n^_j) W over neighbours with x^_ji . n^_i < 0, kept only where v^_i . n^_i >= 0.6 (eqs. 4 to 7), kinetic energy 0.5 m |v|^2, each mapped by the clamping function (eq. 1), and n_d = I_k (k_ta I_ta + k_wc I_wc) dt (eq. 8). (4) A sum pyramid of the counts (Ziegler, Tevs, Theobalt and Seidel 2006). (5) Every pool slot steps: slots the ring head reaches take the next emitted particle, found by walking the pyramid down (no atomics, no scatter), sampled uniformly in the cylinder of radius r_V = radius spanned by x_f and x_f + dt v_f with velocity offset + v_f; the others are classified by fluid neighbour count (under 6 spray, over 20 bubble, else foam) and advected: spray ballistically (Euler-Cromer), foam by the cubic-spline averaged fluid velocity (position only), bubbles by v += dt (-k_b g) + k_d (v~_f - v); only foam ages (section 3.2). (6) The head moves on. Each frame the pool is drawn as camera-facing discs into a premultiplied rgba16float layer, hidden behind the scene, dimmed by Beer-Lambert for the path below the liquid surface, and laid over the liquid in the composite.

Choices the paper leaves open, ours: the surface normal is the W-weighted mean of the unit vectors from the neighbours, and only particles where its length is at least `surfaceThreshold` (0.25) of the weight sum are surface particles; interior particles have no defined normal, get no wave-crest potential and do not count as neighbours in the curvature sum (the paper evaluates curvature on a surface point set). Counts are rounded stochastically, floor(n_d + U) with U from the PCG hash of Jarzynski and Olano (2020), so the expected count is the paper's n_d; at most 255 per particle per step. Lifetimes are mix(min, max, I_k (k_ta I_ta + k_wc I_wc) / (k_ta + k_wc)), so the strongest emitters make the longest-lived foam (the paper sets lifetime "in relation to the generation potentials"). The cylinder radius uses sqrt(X_r) so samples are uniform over the disc, as the paper states they are. When one step emits more than the pool, the emitted particles are subsampled evenly. Particles are drawn as discs; the paper ray-casts a binary density volume offline.

Proof (RTX A6000, native WebGPU and MARGO_COMPAT=1, both pass; packages/water/test/liquid-whitewater.browser.mjs): two 216-particle lattice blocks meeting head-on at 4 m/s emit 232 particles on the first step on the GPU and 232 in the float64 brute-force oracle (packages/water/test/liquid-whitewater-reference.js, no grid); 30 steps run twice give identical counts every step; the WebGL2 layer gives the same counts as WebGPU at every probe; a fluid at rest emits nothing; a 256-slot pool fills and stays at 256. CPU tests check the oracle against a three-particle hand case of equations 1 to 8, that the stochastic rounding is unbiased, and that every whitewater shader checks and translates for the WebGL2 layer with no compute pass (nothing lands on the layer's JavaScript tier). Work: a frame without `update()` adds one sprite pass, one draw and a 160-byte uniform write, no bind group, buffer or texture; a step adds 8 bucket passes, normals, potentials, log2(side) pyramid levels, the update, the head and the sprites (21 more passes at 65,536 particles, 22 at 262,144), plus the velocity upload of a CPU source.

Cost (busy-machine diagnostics, A6000, wall time of one rendered frame with its GPU wait, 256 x 192): a step 3.8 ms at 65,536 fluid particles and a 65,536 pool, 9.0 ms at 262,144 and 262,144; a frame without a step 2.6 and 2.3 ms. WebGL2 layer: 6.9 and 12.0 ms per step. Before the tiled grid (a Teschner hash table) the 262,144 step took 25.5 ms. Memory: two r32uint bucket textures and a depth32float attachment of 4 x cells texels (cells = next power of two of 2 * count, 1,024 to 4,194,304), an rgba32float normal texture and two r32uint pyramid atlases of the fluid side (next power of two of sqrt(count)), four rgba32float pool textures and the rgba16float layer at frame size.

## Narrow band

With `narrowBand`, each `update()` builds the layered neighbourhood of Oliveira and Paiva (2022, Algorithm 1) on the GPU and the depth pass draws only its particles. Render passes only, no compute: (1) bounds by a min/max reduction (four particles per texel, then 2 x 2 levels); (2) a 1 x 1 grid pass: voxel size 2h (`bandVoxel`, default 4 * radius), grown by 1.25 until the grid fits an A x A atlas (A = max(64, fluid side)), starting 0.75 voxel below the lowest particle so lattice spacings of v / 2 or v / 3 never sit on a voxel face; (3) one additive point draw per particle into an rgba16float atlas: n_i and the sum of positions relative to the voxel; (4) layer 1, a full voxel with an empty 26-neighbour or on the grid border; (5) layer 2, a full voxel not in layer 1 with a layer-1 neighbour holding at most n_min = 2^3 = 8 particles. The band B is every particle in a labelled voxel. The depth sprites of particles outside B leave their vertex stage off screen. Thickness: the paper splats voxel centroids weighted by n_j with a Gaussian (eq. 3); ours draws one sphere per full voxel at its centroid with radius radius * cbrt(n_j) (the same volume as its particles), cut at the scene depth as before, so the thickness stays in world units for Beer-Lambert.

Proof (RTX A6000, native WebGPU and MARGO_COMPAT=1, both pass; packages/water/test/liquid-band.browser.mjs): on 8^3-voxel lattice blocks with 2^3 and 3^3 particles per voxel the GPU's voxel, layer-1, layer-2 and band counts equal the JavaScript oracle of Algorithm 1 (packages/water/test/liquid-band-reference.js) on the GPU's own grid: 729 voxels, layer 1 386, layer 2 218 and 53, band 3,096 of 4,096 and 5,994 of 13,824; the nearest depth is identical texel for texel with and without the band (no holes, 6,967 and 7,328 covered texels); the summed thickness differs by 0.02% and 0.04%. CPU tests check the oracle on hand blocks and that every band shader checks and translates for the WebGL2 layer. Work: a steady frame is unchanged (6 passes, 6 draws, 320 B, no bind group); an `update()` adds 10 to 11 passes and 18 to 20 bind groups.

Cost (busy-machine diagnostic, wall per frame with its GPU wait, 262,144 particles in a 64^3 lattice at 1920 x 1080): the band holds 67,032 particles (26%); a steady frame 2.4 ms without and 1.2 ms with it natively, 3.8 and 3.1 ms on the WebGL2 layer; an update frame 3.9 and 3.9 ms natively, 5.0 and 7.0 ms on the layer. The default stays off until the quiet rerun times it on the A6000 and the iGPU.

Sources: F. Oliveira, A. Paiva, Narrow-Band Screen-Space Fluid Rendering, Computer Graphics Forum 41(6) 2022, https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.14510 (the authors' PDF, Algorithm 1 and equation 3).
